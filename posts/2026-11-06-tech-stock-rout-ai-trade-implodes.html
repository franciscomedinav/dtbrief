The AI Trade Imploded on Thursday. Tech Stocks Lost $2 Trillion in a Day.

The Nasdaq fell into correction territory as investors digested the Democrats’ midterm sweep — and the fear that the AI data-center buildout is about to get a lot harder to build.

1 What happened

Wall Street staged a ferocious selloff in tech and artificial intelligence stocks on Thursday, and the numbers are hard to overstate. The tech-heavy Nasdaq fell firmly into correction territory, down as much as 14 percent from its all-time high just a month ago, with some $2 trillion wiped off its market value in a single day. The Philadelphia chip index fell 12 percent, its second-biggest drop on record. SanDisk plunged 22 percent, Micron, AMD, and Marvell each fell about 17 percent, and Nvidia dropped 9 percent. All eleven sectors of the S&P 500 fell.

The trigger was politics. Democrats swept the midterm elections on Tuesday, winning both houses of Congress on a campaign that attacked data-center expansion, and investors spent Thursday repricing what that means for the AI buildout. Voters had already spoken directly: in more than forty local ballot measures this week, they rejected data-center development, and an NBC Election Day poll found 76 percent of voters opposed data centers in their communities. The rout caps a brutal stretch — the Nasdaq 100 shed more than $2.5 trillion in market value during October alone, as investors grew wary of Big Tech’s soaring AI spending.

2 Why it matters

First, the AI trade was priced for a world in which building data centers was an engineering problem, not a political one. Permits, power, water, and community consent are now the binding constraints on the industry’s growth, and every one of them runs through elected officials who just watched voters punish data-center expansion. A chip order can be placed in a day; a data center takes years of approvals.

Second, the money at stake is no longer abstract. Data centers need enormous amounts of electricity and grid upgrades, and the central fight is now over who pays for them. OpenAI’s answer on Thursday was a new National Community Compact promising to pay the full cost of its power and infrastructure — an admission that the industry can no longer assume communities will absorb the cost. If builders have to pay their own way, the economics of the buildout change.

The market just repriced the AI boom for a world where building the infrastructure is a political fight, not just an engineering one.

Third, this reaches beyond Wall Street. The same stocks sit in most retirement accounts and index funds, so a 14 percent Nasdaq slide hits households, not just traders. And the scrutiny is spreading inside the industry itself: Apple is cutting what its engineers can spend on AI tools, a sign that even the world’s most valuable company is watching its AI bill. The boom is entering its second phase, where the question is not how fast you can build, but whether you can afford to — and whether your neighbors will let you.

3 What to watch next

  • Friday jobs data October’s US employment data lands Friday, with economists expecting a net 65,000 rise in payrolls and an unchanged 4.2% unemployment rate. Strong numbers could steady nerves; weak ones deepen the selloff.
  • Washington Watch what Democrats actually propose on data centers — permits, power-cost rules, and community-consent requirements — once the new Congress is seated. Campaign rhetoric becomes legislation, or it doesn’t.
  • The builders’ answer OpenAI’s compact is the first response. Watch whether Amazon, Meta, and Google match it, and whether voters and local officials find the promises convincing enough to lift moratoria.
  • AI spending Earnings season continues. Watch whether Big Tech trims AI capital spending — or doubles down — and whether investors reward restraint or punish it.

The rest of today’s briefing

OpenAI drops data-center NDAs and promises to pay its own power bills — OpenAI’s new National Community Compact says it will stop asking public officials to sign non-disclosure agreements on data-center projects, and will pay the full cost of the electricity its facilities use plus the grid infrastructure to serve them. The company will also publish expected job and hiring figures, share construction plans and noise, water, and energy projections before building, and scale back power draw at peak demand. The move follows Tuesday’s midterms, where an NBC Election Day poll found 76 percent of voters opposed data centers in their communities. The compact covers campuses OpenAI owns and operates; the majority of its capacity, leased or built with partners, is an open question. — The Verge

OpenAI launches GPT-6 Cyber with a Critical cybersecurity rating — OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber, a model built to find and exploit security flaws, and gave it the highest classification in its safety framework. Researchers used it to find 81 previously unknown Firefox vulnerabilities rated high severity, and to develop working exploits for 948 Linux kernel flaws, including 588 that were previously unknown. OpenAI has disclosed 304 critical Linux findings to maintainers with proposed patches; the underlying experiment cost about $1.2 million in API credits. The model is available to a small group of vetted customers through Daybreak Red, its access tier for authorized security work. — Reuters

Meta, Google, and TikTok sue California over its teen social media law — The three companies have each sued California’s attorney general to block a law that restricts features the state deems addictive for users under sixteen. The law, signed in September and set to take effect at the start of the new year, bars autoplay videos and personalized feeds for under-16s and requires platforms to delete those users’ accounts if the features aren’t switched off. The companies argue the restrictions violate their rights, setting up a court fight over how far states can go in regulating teenagers’ social media use. Australia became the first country to ban social media for under-16s altogether in December 2025. — TechCrunch

Claude can now plug into your bank account and explain where your money went — Anthropic launched Personal Finance, a Claude tool that lets US users connect bank and credit card accounts through Plaid and ask plain-language questions about their money. It totals spending across accounts, sorts it by category, and flags duplicate charges, unexpected fees, and steadily rising bills. Access is read-only, so it cannot move, manage, or invest money, and conversations in the hub aren’t used to train its models. It’s rolling out in beta this week to Pro, Max, and Ultra subscribers in the US. — TechCrunch

Apple is cutting what its engineers can spend on AI — Apple lowered the daily AI spending cap for its engineering teams from $300 to $200 in October, with further cuts under consideration and some allowances falling to $100 a day. Outside engineering, the company is reportedly weighing a daily limit as low as $25, though employees can request exceptions. Teams are being pushed toward cheaper models and on-device AI as Apple tries to control internal spending, a notable shift for a company racing to catch up in AI. — Bloomberg

The New York Times is reportedly in talks to buy Letterboxd for $330 million — The Times has entered exclusive negotiations to acquire Letterboxd, the social network for film lovers, according to the Times’ own reporting. Letterboxd has grown from 3 million users in January 2021 to 28 million by April of this year, with more than half of its users under 35. Film studios A24 and Sony were also interested, but A24 was priced out. Letterboxd’s projected earnings for this year are estimated at $15 million, and no deal is final yet. — TechCrunch

Airbnb will launch an AI travel agent in the first half of 2027 — Airbnb announced in its third-quarter shareholder letter that it will roll out an AI travel agent in the first half of 2027, its biggest bet yet on artificial intelligence. Instead of a text-heavy chatbot, the agent will compose a custom interface in real time from a library of hundreds of travel-specific components such as maps, photos, comparisons, calendars, and itineraries. It will also help guests throughout their trip, from changing a reservation to refunds, rebooking, and 24-hour safety support. The announcement came alongside third-quarter revenue up 19 percent to $4.87 billion, topping Wall Street estimates. — TechCrunch

IBM, AMD, and Oak Ridge launch a quantum error correction platform — IBM, AMD, and Oak Ridge National Laboratory launched a joint platform aimed at solving quantum computing’s tendency to make errors, one of the biggest obstacles to making the machines useful. The platform pairs IBM’s quantum computers, including its most powerful chip, the 120-qubit Nighthawk, with Oak Ridge’s Umbra supercomputer, powered by AMD’s Instinct processors. The effort supports a US Department of Energy goal to build a scientifically useful quantum computer by late 2028, though IBM said ongoing experiments are roughly 230 times short of that target. — Reuters

Amazon ditches Grubhub and taps Uber Eats for Prime — Amazon has made Uber Eats its official restaurant delivery partner for Prime members in the US, effectively ending its deal with Grubhub. Prime members can order from Uber Eats directly through Amazon.com and the Amazon Shopping app, with no Uber account needed and no delivery fee on orders over $25. Amazon’s agreement with Grubhub ends Thursday, though members who already activated Grubhub Plus can keep using it through December 31, 2027. Amazon and Uber are offering $20 off Prime members’ first two orders of $25 or more during the first two weeks. — TechCrunch
