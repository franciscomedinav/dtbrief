SpaceX Just Borrowed $40 Billion to Buy AI Chips. Here’s What That Actually Means.

Elon Musk says the company closed $40 billion in mostly callable debt to buy Nvidia graphics chips — and used the announcement to declare the credit window shut for everyone else.

1 What happened

Elon Musk announced on Sunday that SpaceX has closed $40 billion in debt financing to buy graphics processing units from Nvidia. In a post on X, he said the debt is mostly callable and the chips are expected to arrive in the first and second quarters of 2027 — and that the financing is “not a major factor this quarter.”

In plain English: SpaceX just borrowed an enormous sum to buy the chips that power AI, and it structured the loan so it can pay the money back early if it chooses. “Callable” means the lender can’t lock SpaceX in — the company can repay the debt ahead of schedule, which is useful if chip prices fall or it wants to refinance on better terms. The deal had been taking shape for weeks: earlier reports described roughly $10 billion in bank loans plus $30 billion in investment-grade bonds, with Apollo Global Management leading the effort.

On Monday, SpaceX confirmed the structure in a regulatory filing, saying it had entered a binding commitment letter with an entity managed by Apollo that will buy the GPUs and lease them back to SpaceX, with quarterly payments over about five years. The company also said it is bringing roughly 200,000 of Nvidia’s GB300 chips online every month through the end of the year, before shifting to the next-generation Vera Rubin platform, and reported 1.7 gigawatts of installed GPU capacity as of September 30 — up from 0.6 gigawatts a year earlier. Fourth-quarter capital spending is guided at $35 billion to $40 billion.

2 Why it matters

First, the sheer scale. Forty billion dollars for chips alone — not data centers, not power, just the processors — shows that AI compute has become a balance-sheet business. The companies building AI now borrow like governments and utilities, not like software startups.

Debt markets have almost entirely shut off access to money for any company that doesn’t have substantial revenue independent of AI data centers. — Elon Musk

Second, look at who can still borrow. Musk’s real message wasn’t about SpaceX — it was about everyone else. SpaceX could raise the money because it is investment-grade and earns revenue from Starlink and rocket launches, not just from renting out GPUs. Companies that only rent out AI compute — the “neoclouds” like CoreWeave — are facing a credit wall, and Musk predicted more than 200 of them will fail or consolidate.

Third, the chips have somewhere to go. SpaceX’s AI business has become its largest segment, and the company is selling computing capacity to outside customers under multi-year contracts. This debt isn’t a gamble on a future product — it’s financing for hardware that arrives early next year and starts earning right away.

3 What to watch next

  • The final terms The interest rate hasn’t been set and the master lease isn’t final — SpaceX expects to sign it before year-end. Watch whether the final pricing reflects confidence or caution.
  • The deliveries The first GPU shipments are due in the first quarter of 2027. On-time delivery is the difference between a financed build-out and a very expensive IOU.
  • The neocloud shakeout Musk’s prediction of widespread failures among the 200-plus neoclouds is now a testable claim. Watch for emergency financings, covenant breaches, or consolidation among the pure-play GPU renters.
  • The Vera Rubin shift SpaceX plans to move to Nvidia’s next-generation Vera Rubin platform after the GB300 rollout. How fast it pivots will say a lot about how long today’s chips stay valuable.
