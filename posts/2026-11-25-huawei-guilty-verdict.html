Huawei Is Guilty.

A federal jury in Brooklyn convicted Huawei on ten of twelve criminal counts — bank fraud, wire fraud, sanctions violations and stealing trade secrets from American companies. The case began in 2018. Here is what the verdict means, and what to watch.

1 What happened

A federal jury in Brooklyn convicted Huawei and three of its subsidiaries on Tuesday on ten of twelve criminal counts, including bank fraud, wire fraud, conspiracy to steal trade secrets, money laundering and violating United States sanctions on Iran. The verdict closes a criminal case that began in 2018, when the United States first indicted the Chinese telecom giant, and it follows an eleven-week trial in which prosecutors called more than sixty witnesses and the defense called fifteen.

Prosecutors proved a decades-long scheme with two halves. On the financial side, Huawei misled banks — including H S B C, Standard Chartered and B N P Paribas — about its business in Iran, concealing its control of a company called Skycom so it could obtain billions of dollars and access to dollar-clearing services. On the trade-secret side, Huawei conspired to steal intellectual property from American companies over two decades: Cisco router source code, Quintel antenna technology, and technical data about T-Mobile’s phone-testing robot, known as Tappy. In one incident detailed at trial, a Huawei employee slipped a piece of that robot into a backpack. Prosecutors also said Huawei hired a professor to gain access to a circuit board developed by the semiconductor company CNEX.

The jury acquitted Huawei of the two broadest charges, racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice — the claim that the company operated as a criminal enterprise. Huawei says the charges on which it was convicted are without legal or factual merit, that the five trade-secret disputes cited by the government were civil matters resolved over the past two decades, and that it plans to challenge the convictions. Sentencing is set for September fourteenth, twenty twenty-seven, when the company faces criminal fines, forfeiture and restitution.

2 Why it matters

First, this is the end of an eight-year legal saga — and a first. The United States rarely prosecutes a foreign corporation criminally, and almost never one of China’s largest and best-known companies. The verdict is the government’s answer to a question American technology giants have asked for decades: what happens when a competitor is accused of building its business on stolen technology? The jury’s answer, on ten counts, was that it is a crime, not a business dispute.

Second, the trade-secret theft was not abstract — it was specific, and it was aimed at American companies. Prosecutors showed the jury stolen Cisco source code, stolen antenna designs, and a robot component carried out of a T-Mobile lab. Huawei has long argued that its commercial growth came from engineering, investment and research, and that some of the allegedly stolen materials were publicly available. The jury did not buy it. For every American company that has watched a competitor walk out with its designs, the verdict is a signal that the courts will treat that as fraud.

The jury’s verdict was blunt: stealing a competitor’s technology is a crime, not a business dispute.

Third, the timing is the story. The verdict lands days after President Trump visited Huawei’s home city of Shenzhen for a summit with Chinese leader Xi Jinping, and in the same week the United States blacklisted a Singapore firm suspected of diverting Nvidia chips to China. Huawei has been on a United States trade blacklist since 2019, cut off from American technology. The conviction does not change that — but it hardens the line between the two countries’ technology industries, and it will hang over every negotiation about chips, trade and sanctions that follows.

3 What to watch next

  • The sentencing Watch what penalties Judge Ann Donnelly sets on September 14, 2027 — criminal fines, forfeiture and restitution — and whether they bite a company already cut off from American technology.
  • Huawei’s challenge Watch how Huawei challenges the convictions, and whether an appeal narrows the case before sentencing.
  • The U.S.–China tech line Watch whether the verdict hardens Washington’s stance on chip export controls and sanctions enforcement in the months ahead.
  • The chip-diversion cases Watch the Senate Banking Committee hearing on December 3, where Nvidia’s Jensen Huang testifies on export controls, and the Commerce Department’s next moves on firms accused of diverting restricted chips.
