Anthropic’s $97 Debut.

Anthropic opened at $111.50, faded to $97.17, and still closed up 17% on the biggest IPO in history — while the rest of the AI trade sold off. Here is what the debut means, and what to watch.

1 What happened

Anthropic began trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker ANT, after pricing the largest initial public offering on record at $83 a share. The stock opened at $111.50, 34% above the offering price and briefly valuing the Claude maker near $2 trillion, then faded through the afternoon to close at $97.17, up 17% on the day. The shares slipped about 5% in after-hours trading. The offering sold 980 million shares and raised about $81 billion, and at the close Anthropic carried a fully diluted market value of about $1.8 trillion, making it one of the ten most valuable public companies in the world.

The public sale is only part of the haul. A simultaneous $26 billion private placement with Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft, and TSMC brought the total deal to about $107 billion, the largest capital raise on record, topping the $86 billion SpaceX collected in June. Google and Nvidia put in $10 billion each, AMD $2.5 billion, and Broadcom, Micron, and Microsoft $1 billion each, with TSMC adding $500 million. The offering also comes with a staggered release calendar: up to 315 million shares held by employees, board members, and former staff are free to sell on day one, and up to 1.61 billion by February 2.

The debut is also the first ever by a major frontier AI lab, and it pushed total US IPO volume in 2026 to nearly $246 billion, beating the $195 billion record set in 2021. Anthropic disclosed an annualized revenue run rate of $81 billion as of October 31, up $10 billion from the end of September, and revenue of about $17 billion in the third quarter alone. At the same time it disclosed it needs about $70.7 billion to cover compute and infrastructure commitments over the next twelve months — a reminder of how expensive the race it just joined really is.

2 Why it matters

First, the debut is a live stress test of AI valuations — and the first reading is mixed. Anthropic gave back half its opening gain in a single day, and the broader AI trade fell anyway, with the Nasdaq down 2.3% to a seven-month low. A $1.8 trillion valuation for a company whose shares swung 13% from open to close says the market still wants AI exposure, but is getting picky about the price it will pay for it.

Second, the circular money is now public-market business. Google and Nvidia are Anthropic’s investors, its compute suppliers, and its customers, all at once — and Nvidia’s $10 billion is the same company whose chips power Anthropic’s models. As a private company, those relationships were a footnote. As a public company, every deal between them will be disclosed, priced, and questioned by shareholders who want to know whether Anthropic is buying compute at a fair price or funding its own customers.

The largest IPO in history still couldn’t lift the AI trade — the debut was the exception, not the rescue.

Third, Claude now answers to shareholders. Anthropic built its reputation on a safety-first story and a governance structure designed to keep the mission ahead of the money. Going public does not erase that structure — the founders keep 50.1% of voting control and an independent trust can appoint several board members — but it adds a quarterly earnings call, a share price that moves on every product decision, and investors who will judge the company on growth. The seven founders also cashed out about $1.95 billion in the offering, roughly $280 million each, while pledging 80% of their personal equity to philanthropy — pledges the filings describe as voluntary and revocable.

3 What to watch next

  • The after-hours slide Watch whether ANT holds the $97 close when Friday’s trading opens, after slipping about 5% in after-hours trading. Giving back the pop would say the debut day’s excitement has already cooled.
  • The lock-up calendar Watch the supply of shares: up to 315 million are free to sell on day one, and up to 1.61 billion by February 2. Each release date is a test of whether early holders want to cash out.
  • The first public numbers Watch Anthropic’s first quarterly report as a public company. Until now its finances have been a matter of leaks and estimates; the earnings call will be the first hard look at what Claude actually earns — and what its $70.7 billion of compute commitments cost.
  • The sell-off Watch whether the AI rout that hit SoftBank, TSMC, and Samsung deepens into next week, or whether Anthropic’s debut steadies the sector. The debut and the sell-off are the same story: the market is repricing the AI trade in real time.

The rest of today’s briefing

The AI sell-off deepens, with Asian tech stocks plunging and the Nasdaq at a seven-month low — The Nasdaq fell 2.3% on Thursday to its lowest close in seven months, the S&P 500 dropped 2% for a sixth straight down day, and the rout spread to Asia overnight: Japan’s Nikkei plunged 5.1%, Taiwan’s Taiex sank 6.8% for its worst week since 1990, and SoftBank fell about 15% as investors reassessed its roughly $65 billion bet on OpenAI. — Dow Jones

California’s attorney general subpoenas Nvidia over whether China got advanced chips — Attorney General Rob Bonta opened a probe into whether Nvidia’s advanced AI chips or confidential information reached China and whether the company misled investors, following former CEO Jensen Huang’s resignation after reports of his relationship with a woman US authorities suspect of working for Chinese intelligence. — Reuters

The White House fires its national cyber director after a wave of tech outages — Sean Cairncross is out just over a year after taking the job, following a week of failures that hit air travel, banking, Microsoft services, and the National Security Agency. Pentagon CIO Kirsten Davies takes over in an acting capacity, and the Senate must confirm a permanent replacement. — Reuters

Apple is building its own AI agent that can use your Mac like a person — Job listings describe an agent that browses the web, works across apps, runs commands, and writes and debugs code, with a launch eyed as soon as the end of 2027. At the same time, the upcoming macOS 27.3 beta makes it harder for rival agents to take full control of a Mac. — TechCrunch

Nvidia is in advanced talks to buy diffusion AI startup Inception for more than $500 million — Inception’s Mercury diffusion models generate text by refining many tokens in parallel, and the company claims they run up to ten times faster than traditional models. All three co-founders are expected to join Nvidia, and the talks began before Jensen Huang stepped down. — Dealroom

Google DeepMind maps four paths from human-level AI to superintelligence — Research scientist Tim Genewein argues the leap to superintelligence is a technical trajectory already in motion, laying out four compounding pathways — scaling, paradigm shifts, group agents, and recursive self-improvement — and projects a cognitive workload costing $1 billion in compute today could cost just $1,000 within ten years. — DeepMind Institute

Attackers hijack Microsoft’s Agent Framework package on PyPI to steal cloud credentials — Two malicious versions of Microsoft’s agent-framework package, 1.26.1 and 1.26.2, installed a credential stealer that ran every time Python started, harvesting Azure tokens and developer secrets before being quarantined within about an hour. The safe version is 1.26.0. — The Hacker News

Anthropic’s seven founders cashed out nearly $2 billion in the record IPO — Each co-founder sold about 3.4 million shares at $83 apiece, roughly $280 million each before fees and taxes, while pledging 80% of their personal equity to philanthropy — pledges the filings describe as voluntary and revocable. — TechCrunch

The chip-cloud crackdown bites: Nscale faces a license fight and DayOne shelves its $5 billion IPO — Nscale must seek a US license within 90 days to keep renting 2,304 Nvidia B200 GPUs to ByteDance’s Singapore unit, a deal worth 73% of its 2025 revenue, while DayOne paused a US IPO of up to $5 billion and bankers paused a $480 million chip loan. Chinese AI firms now face withdrawal from the Nvidia chips they depend on. — Wall Street Journal
