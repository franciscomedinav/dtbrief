AMD Posted a Record $13.4 Billion Quarter. AI Data Centers Are Now Two-Thirds of Its Business.

Data-center sales nearly doubled to $8.5 billion on AI demand, and AMD guided to $17 billion next quarter — a sign the AI infrastructure boom is still accelerating.

1 What happened

Advanced Micro Devices reported record third-quarter revenue of $13.4 billion, up forty-five percent from a year earlier, beating expectations. The quarter, which ended September 26, was powered by the data-center business, where revenue jumped ninety-seven percent to $8.5 billion. That segment now accounts for about sixty-four percent of AMD's total sales, up from forty-seven percent a year ago.

Two product lines did the heavy lifting: EPYC server processors and the new Instinct MI450 graphics chips, built to run artificial intelligence workloads in data centers. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.09, above the expected $1.95, while the company's gross margin held at fifty-four percent. Not everything rose: personal computer and gaming revenue fell eight percent to $3.7 billion. But the data-center surge more than compensated, and shares jumped about nine percent in after-hours trading after AMD guided to about $17 billion in fourth-quarter revenue, above the roughly $16.3 billion analysts expected.

2 Why it matters

First, this is the clearest evidence yet that AI infrastructure spending is still accelerating, not cooling. For months, investors have worried that the data-center boom might be a bubble, with too much capacity chasing too little revenue. AMD's numbers argue the opposite: data-center revenue nearly doubled, growth quickened through the year, and the company's own forecast for next quarter is higher than Wall Street expected. Chief executive Lisa Su pointed specifically to accelerating demand from AI agents — software that does multi-step work on its own — as a new driver on top of model training.

Data centers went from 47% of AMD's sales to 64% in one year. That is not a side business anymore. It is the business.

Second, AMD is gaining ground in a market long dominated by Nvidia. Its Instinct chips and EPYC processors are now the second credible supplier for the biggest AI buyers, and the Samsung report covered in today's briefing — a new Texas plant making memory parts for AMD and Nvidia — shows the supply chain is being built around both companies. More competition in AI chips should mean more supply, and eventually better prices, for everyone building on top of them.

Third, for ordinary tech users, the takeaway is indirect but real. The same data-center capacity that AMD is selling powers the AI features in phones, search, and workplace software. When that capacity grows, AI features get faster and cheaper to run — which is why your next phone, laptop, or subscription is likely to lean on it even more.

3 What to watch next

  • The $17B test AMD's fourth-quarter guidance is a forecast, not a result. Watch whether the company actually delivers it — and whether data-center growth stays near triple digits.
  • Memory costs Surging memory prices are squeezing the whole industry, including Apple. Watch whether AMD's margins hold as memory gets more expensive.
  • The 2027 claim On the earnings call, Lisa Su said revenue could grow by more than 80% in 2027. That is an ambitious target — watch whether AMD repeats it in future quarters.
  • PC recovery PC and gaming revenue fell 8% this quarter. Watch whether that segment stabilizes, or whether AMD becomes a data-center company with a PC sideline.
