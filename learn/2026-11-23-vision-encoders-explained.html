Vision Encoders, Explained: How a Self-Driving Car Sees the Road

A self-driving car does not see the road the way you do. It watches eight cameras at once, turns the video into a map in its head, and drives from that map. Here is how the vision encoder at the heart of it works — and what Tesla just changed about it.

1 What it is

A vision encoder is the part of a self-driving car’s brain that turns pictures into understanding. The cameras see pixels — millions of colored dots. The encoder is a neural network that reads those pixels and produces a compact summary: where the lanes are, where the other cars are, how fast they are moving, whether that shape on the sidewalk is a pedestrian about to cross. Everything downstream — the planning, the braking, the steering — works from that summary, not from the raw video.

Think of it as the difference between looking at a photograph and describing it to a friend. The photograph is the pixels. The description — “two-lane road, red car ahead slowing down, cyclist on the right” — is the encoder’s output. A good description leaves out the color of the sky and keeps the cyclist. That is exactly what the encoder learns to do: throw away almost everything in the image, and keep the handful of facts that matter for driving.

The encoder matters more than it used to, because the whole industry has moved to end-to-end driving. In the old design, the car was a chain of separate programs: one to spot objects, one to predict where they would go, one to plan a path, one to steer. In the new design, one big neural network learns the whole chain at once, from camera video in to steering commands out — and the vision encoder is the front door of that network. Tesla made the switch with FSD version 12 in 2024, replacing about 300,000 lines of hand-written driving rules with a single learned model, and the new version 15 that started rolling out this weekend upgrades the encoder itself.

2 How it works: the see-encode-predict-drive loop

Every end-to-end driving system runs the same four-step loop, dozens of times per second:

1. See — the cameras capture video. A Tesla uses eight of them: front, front-left, front-right, rear, rear-left, rear-right, and narrow and wide forward views. The network does not look at single snapshots; it watches video streams, because motion — a car pulling out, a pedestrian stepping off the curb — only exists across frames.

1SeeEight cameras capture video of the road in every direction, several frames per second
2EncodeThe vision encoder reads the pixels and builds one shared map of the scene, seen from above
3PredictThe network forecasts what every car, cyclist and pedestrian is about to do
4DriveThe planner picks a path through the forecast, and the car steers, brakes and accelerates to follow it

2. Encode — the vision encoder reads the frames and builds one shared map of the scene, usually as a bird’s-eye view: a top-down grid of the road with every object placed on it. The encoder’s early layers detect simple things — edges, textures, lane paint — and its deeper layers assemble them into cars, people, and road geometry. In Tesla’s design, the same encoder feeds many tasks at once — traffic lights, lane prediction, obstacle detection — a layout called a HydraNet, because many heads share one backbone.

3. Predict — the network forecasts the near future. It has learned, from millions of hours of human driving, how a cyclist wobbling near a parked car usually behaves, and how a car with its brake lights on at a green light usually behaves. The forecast is a set of likely paths for every road user around the car.

4. Drive — the planner picks the car’s own path through the forecast — a curving line of waypoints a few seconds ahead — and a low-level controller turns it into steering, throttle, and brake. The loop then starts again, about 50 times a second.

The clever part is that the whole loop is trained together. In the old modular design, each program was trained separately, so a mistake in perception could not be corrected by the planner — the planner could only work with what it was given. In an end-to-end system, one joint training objective optimizes perception, prediction, and control at the same time, so the encoder learns to keep exactly the details the planner needs, and nothing else.

3 End-to-end vs modular

A modular driving stack is a relay race. The perception module hands a list of objects to the prediction module, which hands forecasts to the planner, which hands a path to the controller. Each handoff is a fixed, human-designed format — a bounding box, a lane curve, a trajectory — and each module is written and tested on its own. When something goes wrong, an engineer can point at the module that failed. The cost is rigidity: information the perception module threw away is gone forever, even if the planner would have wanted it.

An end-to-end driving stack is one brain. Camera video goes in, driving commands come out, and everything in between — the object lists, the lane curves, the forecasts — emerges inside the network’s hidden layers instead of being explicitly programmed. The system is trained by imitation learning: it watches millions of clips of human drivers and learns to copy what competent drivers did, with extra training on the hard clips where drivers had to intervene. Behavior changes arrive as model-weight updates over the air, the way a phone app updates — no code rewrite needed.

ModularSeparate programs for perception, prediction, planning and control, joined by fixed formats. Easier to debug and inspect — but information thrown away at one handoff is gone for good.
End-to-endOne neural network, cameras in and driving out, trained by watching human drivers. More flexible and faster to improve — but its decisions live inside the network, where they are harder to explain.
A modular stack is a relay race with fixed handoffs. An end-to-end stack is one brain that learns the whole drive at once.

4 Why everyone’s building them now

Two stories in today’s briefing are the same story. Tesla began rolling out Full Self-Driving version 15 on Saturday, built around a new vision encoder that is shared between FSD, the Robotaxi service, and the Optimus humanoid robot, and trained on millions of internet videos. Tesla claims the new system handles safety-critical situations 25 times better — though it has published no data backing that figure — and the headline feature is Tesla Reality View, a live generative visualization that shows the driver what the network plans to do.

The sharing is the point. One encoder, trained once, now serves three products: the cars, the robotaxis, and a robot that walks. When the encoder gets better at reading a street scene, all three get better at once. That is why the same architecture is spreading across the industry: Waymo’s driver, the robotaxi fleets in China, and the research labs all run variations of the same idea — cameras in, a shared scene representation in the middle, driving out.

And the encoder is where the money goes. Training it takes fleets of cars collecting video, data centers full of GPUs, and the hard clips — the near-misses and interventions — that teach the network what ordinary driving never shows it. Tesla says it trained on roughly 10 million fleet clips, weighted toward the hard ones. The company with the most real-world driving video has the best teacher, and the encoder is where that advantage shows up.

5 The honest part

First, cameras have to guess depth, and sometimes they guess wrong. A camera-only system infers how far away things are from the way they move across frames — depth from parallax — instead of measuring it directly the way lidar does. That works well in daylight and fails more often in rain, glare, fog, and darkness, which is exactly when driving is hardest. Waymo and most rivals hedge the bet with lidar and radar; Tesla does not.

Second, imitation learning copies human mistakes as faithfully as human skill. The network learns from what drivers did, including the sloppy parts, and it only learns the situations that appear in its training video. A rare event — a mattress flying off a truck, a child chasing a ball between parked cars — may simply never have appeared in the clips. That is why the systems are still supervised: FSD version 15 remains a driver-assistance feature, and the driver must stay ready to take over.

Third, the 25-times claim is unproven. Tesla has published no data comparing version 15 with human drivers, or with its own previous version, behind the “25x better handling of safety-critical cases” line. The honest summary: the vision encoder is the most important piece of the self-driving stack, the end-to-end bet is now the industry’s default, and the technology is real — but the safety claims still need the data, and the driver is still part of the system.

Key terms

  • Vision encoder The neural network that turns raw camera video into a compact summary of the road — where the lanes, cars, and people are — for the rest of the driving system to work from.
  • End-to-end driving A driving system where one neural network learns the whole chain, from camera video in to steering commands out, instead of chaining separate programs together.
  • Bird’s-eye view (BEV) A top-down map of the road, built from the cameras, with every road user placed on it. The encoder’s main product, and the map the car drives from.
  • Imitation learning Training a driving network by showing it millions of clips of human drivers and teaching it to copy what competent drivers did.
  • HydraNet / multi-task learning A network layout where many task-specific heads — traffic lights, lanes, obstacles — share one feature-extraction backbone, so the work is done once instead of once per task.
  • Critical intervention rate How often the driver had to take over from the system. The rate Tesla uses to measure safety: fewer interventions per mile means a better driver.
