Quantum Computing, Explained: A Computer That Tries Every Path at Once

A computer that tries every path at once — and why Europe just bet €50 billion that it is the next great technology race.

1 What it is

Quantum computing is a new kind of computer that uses the laws of quantum mechanics — the physics of the very small — to solve certain problems far faster than any classical machine. A normal computer stores information as bits, each one a zero or a one. A quantum computer stores information as qubits, quantum bits, and a qubit can be a zero, a one, or a blend of both at the same time. That blend is called superposition, and it is the heart of the whole idea.

The classic comparison makes the scale vivid. A classical computer with 300 bits holds one 300-bit number at a time. A quantum computer with 300 qubits can work with a superposition spanning two to the power of 300 states — more than the number of atoms in the observable universe. The idea has a precise birthday: in 1981, the physicist Richard Feynman argued that simulating quantum systems on classical computers is exponentially hard, so we should build computers out of quantum systems themselves. Today IBM, Google, IonQ and a dozen startups are racing to build machines with thousands of reliable qubits.

Two more quantum effects do the work. Entanglement links qubits so that measuring one instantly determines the outcome of measuring another, no matter how far apart they are — it is what makes many qubits more powerful than the sum of their parts. And quantum interference lets the wrong answers cancel out, the way waves do, while the right answers reinforce each other. Interference is the engine of every quantum algorithm.

2 How it works: the encode-manipulate-interfere-measure loop

Every quantum computation runs the same four-step loop:

1. Encode — you load the problem into qubits and put them into superposition, a blend of many possible states at once. Where a classical computer tries one path at a time, the quantum computer holds many paths in play simultaneously.

1EncodeLoad the problem into qubits, putting them into a superposition of many possible states at once
2ManipulateApply quantum gates that transform the superposition, exploring many paths in parallel
3InterfereUse interference so the wrong answers cancel and the right answers reinforce each other
4MeasureMeasure the qubits, collapsing the superposition into a single classical answer you can read

2. Manipulate — quantum gates transform the superposition. Gates are the quantum equivalent of the logic gates in a classical chip, but they operate on probability amplitudes, the numbers that say how likely each outcome is. Algorithms such as Shor’s, from 1994, and Grover’s, from 1996, are recipes of gates for specific problems — factoring large numbers, and searching unstructured data.

3. Interfere — the amplitudes are tuned so that the paths leading to wrong answers cancel each other out and the paths leading to right answers add up. This is the step that turns “many paths at once” into a useful answer instead of noise.

4. Measure — you read the qubits, and the superposition collapses into one classical result. Measurement is probabilistic, so real programs run the same computation many times and take the most common answer.

3 Classical vs quantum

A classical computer is a superb generalist. It runs one instruction at a time, very fast, on bits that are definitely zero or definitely one. Everything you use daily — your phone, your laptop, the servers behind every website — is classical, and for most tasks it will always be the right tool. A quantum computer is not a faster version of it. It is a different machine for a different shape of problem.

A quantum computer attacks problems whose solution spaces are exponentially large: factoring the huge numbers that protect encryption, simulating molecules for drug discovery, optimizing routes and portfolios across millions of combinations. For these, no amount of classical speed helps, because the number of possibilities grows faster than any chip can count. Quantum computing restructures the computation itself, using superposition and interference to explore the space in a fundamentally different way.

Classical computerBits are 0 or 1, one instruction at a time, extremely fast. The right tool for almost everything — documents, video, websites, games, databases. It will always be the generalist.
Quantum computerQubits hold blends of 0 and 1, exploring many paths at once through superposition, entanglement and interference. Built for a narrow set of problems — factoring, molecular simulation, optimization — where the solution space is exponentially large.
A classical computer is a faster abacus. A quantum computer is a different kind of machine for a different shape of problem.

4 Why everyone’s building it now

This week Europe made the biggest bet yet. The European Commission proposed a fifty billion euro sovereign tech equity fund, aimed mainly at A I and quantum companies, alongside a draft Quantum Act that would favor European suppliers in public procurement. The draft targets about one hundred error-corrected logical qubits per system by 2030, or soon after, scaling toward thousands per platform by 2035. It is part of the Quantum Europe Strategy, adopted in July 2025, which aims to make Europe a global quantum leader by 2030. Six EuroHPC quantum computers are already operational or being deployed across the continent, and the Commission projects the quantum sector will exceed a global value of one hundred fifty-five billion euros by 2040.

The hardware race is just as active. IBM’s Heron processor runs 156 qubits and Google’s Willow chip runs 105, both from 2024, and both are powerful enough to demonstrate quantum advantage on specific tasks. The reason governments care is not only computing power. A quantum computer large enough to run Shor’s algorithm could break the public-key encryption that protects banking, messaging and state secrets — which is why the draft Quantum Act also introduces quantum-safe purchasing requirements for critical sectors.

And the demand side is already visible in the news. Just this week, Google DeepMind’s A I agents used formal verification to design faster quantum algorithms, cutting the cost of simulating fusion by 11.8 times. The two technologies are converging: A I designs the quantum circuits, and quantum computers may one day run the simulations A I cannot.

5 The honest part

First, qubits are fragile. A qubit holds its superposition only while it is isolated from the world, and the slightest vibration, heat or stray electromagnetic field collapses it — a problem called decoherence. That is why superconducting quantum processors run at about fifteen thousandths of a degree above absolute zero, colder than outer space, inside elaborate refrigeration. Today’s machines are powerful enough to demonstrate quantum advantage on specific tasks, yet fragile enough that error correction remains the defining challenge of the field.

Second, most problems do not need a quantum computer at all. Quantum machines are not faster at browsing, rendering video, or running a spreadsheet. They help only on problems with the right structure, and for the vast majority of computing, classical chips will always win on cost, speed and reliability. Anyone promising a quantum laptop is selling science fiction.

Third, the timeline is measured in decades, not product cycles. The jump from today’s hundred-qubit machines to the thousands of error-corrected logical qubits needed to break encryption or simulate useful molecules is an engineering climb, not a single breakthrough. The honest summary: quantum computing is real, it has already proved itself on narrow problems, and the race to make it practical is exactly why Europe is spending fifty billion euros — but the machine that changes your daily life does not exist yet.

Key terms

  • Qubit The basic unit of quantum information. Where a classical bit is 0 or 1, a qubit can be 0, 1, or a blend of both at the same time, until it is measured.
  • Superposition The quantum state in which a qubit holds a blend of all its possible states at once, with weights called amplitudes. Measurement collapses it to a single outcome.
  • Entanglement A link between qubits in which measuring one instantly determines the outcome of measuring another. It is what makes many qubits more powerful than the sum of their parts.
  • Quantum interference The wave-like effect in which the probability amplitudes of wrong answers cancel out and right answers reinforce each other. It is the engine of every quantum algorithm.
  • Decoherence The loss of a qubit’s superposition when it interacts with its environment — heat, vibration or stray fields. The reason quantum processors run near absolute zero.
  • Logical qubit A qubit built from many error-prone physical qubits, with error correction keeping it stable. Roughly 100 logical qubits per system is the EU’s 2030 target; breaking encryption would need thousands.
