Neoclouds, Explained: The GPU Landlords Renting Compute to the AI Boom

The AI boom needs more computing power than Amazon, Microsoft, and Google can sell — so a new kind of cloud company showed up to rent it out. Here is how neoclouds work, who pays for them, and why the money behind them worries a Federal Reserve governor.

1 What it is

Every AI model runs on computing power, and the most powerful models need thousands of specialised chips, called GPUs, working together in one place. Amazon, Microsoft, and Google sell that kind of computing, but their clouds were built for everything: email, databases, websites, office software. GPUs are one product line among hundreds. A neocloud is a cloud company built the other way around: its entire business is renting out GPU power for AI, and nothing else.

The name is new, but the companies are not obscure anymore. CoreWeave, the purest example, went public in March 2025 and now rents GPU clusters to AI labs, hyperscalers, and big enterprises. Nebius, spun out of Yandex’s international cloud business and relisted on the Nasdaq in 2024, does the same from data centers in Europe and North America. Others in the category include Lambda, Crusoe, and IREN, and Nvidia holds equity in the three biggest listed names.

Think of it this way. A hyperscaler is a department store: it sells a little of everything, including GPUs. A neocloud is a specialist shop that sells only one thing — AI compute — and it can move faster on the things that matter for that one thing: signing power deals, standing up data centers, and getting the newest chips racked and billing.

2 How it works: the loop

A neocloud works as a loop, and the loop is the whole business:

1. Secure — power and land come first, because a data center without electricity is a warehouse. A neocloud locks in gigawatts of power and sites, often years ahead of the chips. 2. Buy — GPUs by the tens of thousands, bought directly from Nvidia, often before general availability. 3. Contract — multi-year deals with AI labs and hyperscalers, with the customer paying up front or committing for years. 4. Finance — those prepayments, plus loans backed by the contracts themselves, pay for the next round of building. The loop only works if every step holds: power arrives on time, chips arrive on time, and the customer keeps paying.

The network matters as much as the chips. Training a large model needs thousands of GPUs talking to each other at memory speed, so neocloud data centers are built around InfiniBand or high-bandwidth RoCE networking and NVLink connections between chips, not the standard Ethernet a normal cloud uses. That is why a neocloud can sell a cluster a hyperscaler would not sell you as a self-service product.

3 Neocloud vs hyperscaler

A hyperscaler like Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, or Google Cloud offers hundreds of services — storage, databases, security, office tools — and GPU capacity is one line in that catalogue. It has the breadth, the compliance certifications, and the enterprise sales relationships built over decades.

A neocloud has one product and moves faster on it. It negotiates directly with Nvidia for large GPU allocations, often before general availability, and it can stand up a data center for one workload without running a whole general-purpose platform alongside it.

The trade-off is the point. If you need a GPU cluster plus a mature managed database plus enterprise support in a dozen regulatory jurisdictions, a hyperscaler is the more complete answer. If you need the newest chips, fast, in bulk, a neocloud is the faster route — and that is exactly what the AI labs needed.

4 Why everyone’s building it now

The news peg is Nebius. On Thursday it told investors it plans to spend $40 to $45 billion building AI infrastructure in 2027 — far more than Wall Street expected — and said prepayments plus asset-backed financing should cover up to 85 percent of the bill. Its shares fell 17 percent anyway, because the plan is already showing cracks: its biggest customer, Microsoft, is paying for capacity at a New Jersey data center now months behind schedule under a $19.4 billion contract.

CoreWeave, the category leader, is playing the same game. It reported third-quarter revenue of $3.66 billion, up 168 percent, and expects about $9 billion in customer prepayments and deposits over the next three months. Its chief executive says the company will not use the unsecured debt market at current yields, and will instead borrow against specific customer contracts. The backlog behind that borrowing is $132.5 billion.

There is also an accounting reason hyperscalers use neoclouds at all. A hyperscaler that rents capacity from a neocloud keeps the spending off its own balance sheet, while the neocloud carries the debt and the depreciation. The demand driver persists even if GPU supply loosens — and it is why Nvidia itself holds equity in the big listed neoclouds, including a 9.3 percent stake in Nebius.

5 The honest part

Here is the honest part. The loop only works if everything arrives on time, and it does not always. Nebius cut its 2026 revenue forecast by $200 million because third-party sites like New Jersey are late, and only just over half of the Microsoft capacity is now due by year-end. Delays are, in its chief executive’s words, part of the industry right now.

The debt is the bigger worry. CoreWeave carries tens of billions in recourse debt and paid $846 million in quarterly interest in the third quarter, on a $709 million net loss. A Federal Reserve governor warned this week that AI-related debt is on track to exceed $800 billion in 2026, with off-balance-sheet obligations carrying potential payments of more than three times that amount — and that complex financing, including suppliers taking equity stakes in the companies buying their products, makes counterparty risk harder to assess.

So the state of neoclouds in 2026 is: the model works while demand and prices hold, and the customers keep paying up front. If demand cools, or a big customer cancels, the companies are left holding data centers, debt, and chips that lose value fast. That is the bet the whole category is making — and the reason its shares fall hard on any sign of a crack.

Key terms

  • Neocloud A cloud provider built specifically to rent out GPU capacity for AI training and inference, rather than general-purpose computing.
  • Hyperscaler A giant general-purpose cloud — Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud — offering hundreds of services, of which GPU capacity is one.
  • GPU cluster Thousands of graphics processors linked together so they can train one AI model as a single machine.
  • Prepayment Money a customer pays before the capacity is delivered — the cash neoclouds use to fund construction.
  • Asset-backed loan A loan secured against a specific customer contract, so the lender is repaid from that contract’s payments.
  • InfiniBand A high-speed networking technology that lets GPUs in a cluster talk to each other far faster than standard Ethernet.
